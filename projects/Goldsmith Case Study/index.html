Goldsmith

Goldsmith: AI design plus a marketplace for custom jewelry

Goldsmith lets a buyer describe a piece of jewelry in plain words, see it in 3D, and compare quotes from manufacturers. I started it as a Carnegie Mellon Lean Entrepreneurship course project and owned the product, the research plan, the business model and the financial model behind an investor pitch in December 2024, then took it from concept to launch in 12 weeks. This page shows the thinking, the plan and what I would test next. I have not published usage or revenue numbers here, and the plan figures below are targets, not results.

Overview

Custom jewelry is expensive mostly because of the people between you and the person who makes it. Jewelers hold the relationships with manufacturers and designers, and they mark prices up to cover that, plus the store and the hours of consulting that custom work takes. A buyer can’t tell whether a price is fair, so many don’t trust the process, and custom pieces stay out of reach for anyone who isn’t already wealthy. I described Goldsmith as the Warby Parker of custom jewelry.

I wrote the product plan, the research plan and the business model, built the financial model, and presented the plan to investors in December 2024.

What this page covers. I have not published usage numbers for the launch and I won’t invent any. What the page does show is how I went from a pricing problem to a two sided product, the decisions I made, the plan I put in front of investors, and the one assumption I would test before building anything else.

Figures marked as targets come from the plan. Figures marked as research come from public sources.

Problem and research

Three things about the market stood out. The first is price: I found that custom work is marked up by 200% to 500%, and the buyer sees only the final number. The second is trust: most people don’t buy custom jewelry often enough to know what a fair price looks like, so they either overpay or give up. The third is access: a small jeweler without a strong network of verified manufacturers, or without expensive hardware for 3D design, can’t compete on speed or choice.

How big is it?

The honest answer is that nobody agrees on the size of the custom slice. Published estimates of all jewelry sales in 2024 sit at about $367 billion to $371 billion. Estimates for custom jewelry services alone run from about $1.09 billion to $3.63 billion depending on the publisher, and broader definitions of customized jewelry reach $31.8 billion to $36.2 billion. The gap comes from what each report counts, so I treated the range as a reason to size the opportunity from the bottom up, using manufacturers and buyers I could actually reach, and not from a market report.

Measure (2024) Estimate What it counts
All jewelry sales $367B to $371B Two aggregator estimates of global jewelry revenue
Custom jewelry services $1.09B to $3.63B Services only, and the range depends on the publisher
Customized jewelry products $31.8B to $36.2B Broader definition that includes made to order products

Who it's for

The business has three sides, and each one has a different reason to use it. I wrote them down early because they decide what the product has to do.

1

Buyers

People who want a custom piece without knowing how to design one. They want to describe what they like, see it, and know the price they’re given is fair. They will pay for quality but want to compare more than one offer.

2

Jewelers and designers

Small and mid size shops without powerful computers or a deep network of verified manufacturers. They want faster designs, a design department that costs less, and a shorter route to production.

3

Manufacturers

Makers who want custom work to fill capacity. Custom jewelry takes a lot of manual effort to conceive, and leads that arrive with a finished design and a specification make it more profitable.

How I planned to test the idea

I wrote a customer discovery interview guide. It starts from a stated problem and value hypothesis, then asks buyers ten questions: how often they buy, what hurts most about custom jewelry, how much they trust a jeweler’s price on a 1 to 10 scale, whether they would design with natural language, whether they would choose a piece from a 3D preview, and how long they would wait. I built it so the answers would turn into simple probability models and descriptive statistics, not a pile of quotes.

I don’t have interview results to report, and I won’t imply that I do. The guide is the work product.

What already exists

I looked at the tools jewelers use to design. They are all CAD software, and the closest existing route to a finished piece belongs to one company: Gemvision’s MatrixGold is owned by Stuller, which also makes custom jewelry from submitted designs, but only for businesses with a resale account.

Tool What it is Who can use it
MatrixGold (Gemvision, a Stuller company) 3D jewelry design software. Designs can go to Stuller’s CAD/CAM service for casting and finishing. Jewelers with a Stuller resale account
RhinoGold Jewelry specific version of the general purpose Rhino 3D modeler Paid software, mostly trained designers
JewelCAD Pro Proprietary jewelry CAD, listed for Windows Paid software, mostly trained designers
Blender Free, open source mesh modeler that needs add-ons for jewelry work Anyone willing to learn it

In my 2024 review none of these paired design from a text description with a marketplace where manufacturers compete on the same file. That is the gap I built Goldsmith around. It is a claim from my review, not a survey of every tool on the market.

Solution

Goldsmith has two parts, a design tool and a marketplace, and they depend on each other. The tool creates the design a manufacturer can quote on. The marketplace turns that design into a real piece at a price the buyer can compare.

How a buyer would use it

  1. 1DescribeThe buyer writes what they want in plain language.
  2. 2GenerateThe platform creates candidate 3D designs using generative CAD on open source design software.
  3. 3RefineThe buyer iterates, or a jeweler with the full tool edits by hand on cloud GPUs.
  4. 4Request quotesThe design goes to manufacturers, who quote through a request for quotation.
  5. 5Try it onThe buyer receives 3D printed models of their top candidates.
  6. 6OrderThe buyer picks a final design and expects the finished piece in about three weeks. That is the plan, not a measured time.

Decisions I made

Open source design software underneath

I built on open source CAD so one person could move quickly instead of writing a modeler from scratch. The AI work sits on top of it.

Set aside: Licensing or building a proprietary modeler. It would have used most of the time before a buyer saw anything.

Train on data that isn't someone else's design

The plan was to train the models on non IP data and let a buyer protect any design they create. A jeweler’s catalog is their livelihood, and a tool that copies it would not get used by the people I needed.

Set aside: Training on scraped catalogs because it would have been faster.

Verify manufacturers before they can bid

Manufacturers share business documentation before they can quote, and certifications such as the Responsible Jewellery Council’s are shown where a company holds one. That is what makes a buyer willing to compare offers from strangers.

Set aside: An open marketplace where anyone can list, which is easier to grow and harder to trust.

Document the stone, not just the setting

A center stone should come with a lab grading report where it is eligible. GIA, for example, grades and reports on stones; it does not certify manufacturers, so the platform keeps the two separate.

Set aside: Letting a listing imply that a manufacturer is ‘GIA certified’.

Cloud GPUs for the tools

Jewelers and designers get the full tool on cloud GPUs, so a small shop doesn’t need expensive hardware to compete.

Set aside: A local install only, which would have kept out the shops the product was built for.

Let a buyer hold the design first

A 3D printed model of the top candidates lets a buyer judge scale and fit, and it makes the three week wait easier to accept because they have already seen the thing.

Set aside: Renders only, which are cheaper and don’t answer whether the buyer will commit.

How it makes money

The plan had five revenue streams, each tied to a different customer. They were not meant to start at once.

Revenue stream Who pays What the plan says
Software licences and subscriptions Jewelers and designers Licences from $5,000 a year for on premise use, cloud subscriptions from $8,000 a year
Support contracts Business customers Design support engineers and technical support engineers
Platform fees Jewelers, manufacturers and power users A monthly fee based on usage and premium functions
Sponsored listings Manufacturers A fee to appear as sponsored when buyers compare quotes
Transaction fee Marketplace orders A fee on each completed order

How I planned to reach each side

Buyers come in through the website and social media. Jewelers and manufacturers are reached through LinkedIn, trade expos, follow up calls and account managers, with HubSpot connecting the channels. I expected social media outreach to be the cheapest per contact and account managers to matter most for jewelers and manufacturers, since those are relationships, not clicks.

The first version I would build

I wouldn’t build the whole platform first. A first version needs to answer one question: will a buyer pick a final design from a 3D preview and wait about three weeks for it? So I would scope it to a small run.

  • Design. Text to 3D candidates for one category, such as rings, with a small catalog of styles.
  • Quotes. A request for quotation sent by hand to a few manufacturers that publish their services.
  • Try on. 3D printed models of the top candidates for each buyer.
  • Measure. Whether buyers commit, how long they take, and what they would pay.

Where it stands

Goldsmith went from a concept to a business model, a financial model, an investor pitch and a launch in 12 weeks. I have not published launch results on this page, so every figure under the plan below is a target, not an outcome. I would rather say that plainly than dress up targets as results.

What the plan targeted

At the December 2024 pitch I presented a plan for a seed round. These are targets from that plan, not results.

Stage Target in the plan
Year one Beta launch with 1,000 users and 20 manufacturers, and about $500K in revenue
Year two 5,000+ users and partnerships with 50 manufacturers, and about $2M in revenue
Year three 10,000+ users and global manufacturing partnerships, $5M+ in revenue at 90% retention
Long term Establish Goldsmith as the leading custom jewelry design platform, with revenue growing past $20M a year

The assumption I would test first

The plan rests on one behavior. Buyers will choose a final design from a 3D preview and wait about three weeks for the finished piece. If they won’t, the cost advantage disappears, because a jeweler’s fitting room is the thing I was trying to replace. So I would run the small version above with a few manufacturers and a few dozen real buyers before building more of the platform.

Before a run like that I would write down what counts as a pass. The criteria below are proposals I would set in advance, not findings.

What I'd measure Why it matters A pass I'd set before starting
Share of buyers who pick a final design from a 3D preview It tests the core behavior directly A clear majority of buyers who reach the preview stage
Buyers who accept a three week wait The timeline is part of the promise Most buyers who pick a design go on to order
Quote competitiveness It tests the claim that buyers get a better deal Quotes beat what the same buyer was quoted by a jeweler
Manufacturer response and win rate Without supply the marketplace has nothing to sell Manufacturers respond to most requests and win some

The two priorities I set

My business model work ended with two priorities to validate, and I still think they were right.

1

Customer segments

I believed there were more segments than I had found and that some deserved more emphasis, depending on which side of the network they sat. The plan was to map the market for both sides and rank segments by how well they fit the product and the outreach.

2

Key resources and IP

Whether AI generated designs can be protected, and how the data behind the models is governed, was an open legal question. The plan was to research IP law, data governance, open source model datasets and their licensing before training anything.

What I took from it

  • Say what the stage is. A pitch can carry a plan without pretending the plan is a result. Investors trust a founder who keeps those apart.
  • Pick the assumption that can kill the idea. For Goldsmith it was whether buyers will commit from a 3D preview. I would test it before polishing the rest.
  • A marketplace has two cold starts. Buyers won’t come without manufacturers and manufacturers won’t come without buyers. Verification, and handling the first quotes by hand, are how I would start both.
  • Treat legal questions as product questions. Who owns an AI generated design shapes what data can be used for training and what can be promised to a buyer.
  • Match the funding stage to the evidence. Before there is revenue or a working product, the honest ask is an early round such as pre seed or seed, sized to the test above.

What I'd do differently

  • Put a rough version in front of real buyers and manufacturers before the pitch, so the plan had at least one real data point behind it.
  • Start the IP and data governance research in the first weeks, because it constrains the training data and the promise to a buyer.
  • Run the three week promise as an experiment before building the platform that depends on it.
