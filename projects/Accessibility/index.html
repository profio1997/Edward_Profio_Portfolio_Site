Workday Accessibility AI Strategy

Workday's accessibility team wanted to know where AI could make the product work better for people with disabilities, and which companies to build that with. I worked on it for 12 months as an intern, as part of a four-person Carnegie Mellon Tepper capstone team. I led the research into the solutions, and I wrote the rollout plan, the roadmap and the AI development practices that came out of it.

Overview

Workday is the system a large company uses to pay people, track time off, hire and report on its workforce, which means people with disabilities use it every week. The accessibility team had a practical question. AI was making new kinds of help possible, like describing a chart to someone who can’t see it or captioning a meeting live, but the market was crowded and changing monthly. Where should Workday use AI first, and should it build those features or partner with someone who already had?

My team of four from Carnegie Mellon’s Tepper School worked on that question with Workday for 12 months. We had a software lead from a space company, a CEO and business-alliance director, a senior software consultant, and me. I led the research into the solutions. Once we had a recommendation I wrote the rollout plan for each option, a roadmap for when to do what, and a set of practices for how Workday’s accessibility AI team should build with AI.

The brief and how we ran it

We agreed five goals with Workday at the start. Gain a comprehensive understanding of the accessibility technology landscape. Align that landscape to Workday’s strategic objectives. Create a market map, in the style of a CB Insights map, in Figma. Run a trade study of AI accessibility options. And identify strategic opportunities for Workday.

The 12 months fell into five stages, each of which fed the next:

  1. 1FrameAgree goals, split users into four groups, write the job each group needs done
  2. 2MapFind every relevant company, product and research group; build the market map
  3. 3ScoreRate each shortlist on the same five criteria
  4. 4Deep-diveCompare the leaders in each group, including pros, cons and risks
  5. 5RecommendChoose partners, then write the rollout plan, roadmap and AI development practices

I kept regular check-ins going with Workday’s design, engineering and leadership contacts and presented to leaders and customers along the way, so the final recommendations didn’t land as a surprise. That mattered as much as the analysis. A recommendation nobody has seen coming is a recommendation that gets ignored.

Problem and research

Starting from people, not technology

The first thing we did was resist the urge to list vendors. We split accessibility into four groups of users and wrote down, for each, the job they need done and what has to be true for software to do it. It kept us from chasing whichever model was newest, and it gave Workday a way to compare options that wasn’t about features.

Scoring more than 50 vendors

The market map, shown at the top of this page, was the first big deliverable. We found companies, products and research groups in each of the four groups, from large technology firms to university labs and small startups, and sorted them by the kind of help they offered. Then came the harder part: comparing them fairly.

Every shortlist was rated on the same five criteria:

Criterion Why it matters What we looked for
Feature success rate Accessibility users rely on the output; a wrong answer is a harm, not an inconvenience Published accuracy, benchmarks, hands-on testing
Daily and monthly active users Evidence that real people adopt it Usage, retention signals, customer references
Integration compatibility and time Engineering cost and fit with Workday’s platform APIs, data flows, security review effort, estimated weeks
Feature latency Real-time jobs like captions break when they lag Response time under realistic load
Prototyping potential Can we test it with users in weeks, not quarters? Sandbox access, licensing, partner willingness

We showed the result as a table of partial-fill circles, one row per product and one column per criterion, so a busy executive could read a shortlist at a glance. Whenever scores were close, the margin was in the details, which is why we ran deep-dives on the leaders.

What we found in each group

Visual: chart interpretation. We chose chart interpretation as the focus because charts are common in business reporting, and they are one of the places screen readers struggle most. Any solution has to do three things: convert a chart in any format into data points, answer the user’s questions and draw insights from the data, and communicate the answers in a non-visual way.

Two approaches stood out. VizAbility, a research tool from Boston College, is fully integrated and had a high success rate with accuracy fairly even across chart types, and it follows standard accessibility principles. The downsides were that its white paper doesn’t say which language model it uses, it only works with JavaScript chart libraries, and it has trouble classifying contextual questions. The other approach was to pair the open-source WebPlotDigitizer with a language model. That works on every chart type and format, is completely open source, and its LLM integrations were in development, but there was little performance data, many chart types needed manual setup, and the current version needed us to integrate a language model ourselves. Build-versus-buy was a real trade-off, and we didn’t pretend one side won on every axis.

Physical. The job here is to let people control devices and finish tasks without help. We looked at hands-free control (Microsoft Eye Control, which uses eye tracking and has large-scale adoption) and at task automation for people with motor impairments (Liftware, which personalises help with daily routines). Our reasoning for pairing them was that they complement each other: one changes how a person interacts, the other reduces how much they have to do.

Neurodivergent. We assessed two products with very different risk profiles. Axios HQ uses AI to turn dense information into a short, digestible format, which could help people with dyslexia, but private internal memos might surface across the platform. Mentra matches neurodivergent people with jobs in tech that suit their strengths, but many people are not comfortable telling their employer about a neurodivergence.

Hearing. Hard-of-hearing users need accurate real-time transcription in many languages. Manual and semi-automated services exist, but they’re limited in accuracy, reliability and supported languages. Our standout was an open-source agentic framework from Hugging Face, which benchmarked at 90%+ lower cost than enterprise models on tasks like transcription. We also assessed AssemblyAI’s audio-intelligence APIs, which are strong on detecting key attributes of transcribed content. That cost gap changes the build-or-buy answer for this group, which is why it sits further down the roadmap while we verified accuracy and latency.

Solution

The recommendation

Out of more than 50 vendors we surfaced 8 partnerships worth pursuing and recommended three to start with: VizAbility for chart descriptions, Mentra for strengths-based job matching, and Liftware for people with limited mobility. We put the three at the top because each had a strong score on the criteria, each addressed a real job, and each could be piloted without a large integration project.

How the team should build with AI

The last piece was less about vendors and more about how Workday’s own accessibility AI product team should work, so the plan didn’t depend on us being in the room. I wrote it as six practices, in the order a feature moves from idea to release.

  1. 1Bring users in earlyPeople with disabilities in discovery, not only in testing
  2. 2Agree the measuresTask success, accuracy, latency and WCAG conformance
  3. 3Test before releaseAccuracy sets per input type; review of failure cases and bias
  4. 4Build in the safeguardsModel and data disclosure, privacy review, opt-in, a human fallback
  5. 5Roll out in stagesFeature flags, pilot groups and clear rollback triggers
  6. 6Keep watchingUsage data and user feedback; a regression blocks the release

The risks we wrote down next to each option

Responsible AI
VizAbility

Doesn’t disclose its model and only supports JavaScript chart libraries. We made disclosure and accuracy on Workday’s chart types conditions of the pilot.

Mentra

Many people don’t want their employer to know they are neurodivergent. Opt-in only, with the candidate in control.

Axios HQ

Its summaries could help readers with dyslexia, but private memos might surface across the platform. Not recommended without data isolation.

Open-source transcription

Much cheaper, but needs more setup and integration. Test against latency and accuracy targets before committing.

Results

What happened

Over the 12 months we presented the work to Workday’s design, engineering and executive leaders and to customers, with the market map, the trade study, a partnership evaluation matrix, a research report and the recommendation. It informed the direction of Workday’s accessibility AI roadmap, and the full deck is linked at the top of this page.

Looking back

  • Scoring every option on the same five criteria is what made 50+ vendors comparable. It also made the recommendation easy to challenge, which is what leadership needed.
  • Listing each option’s risks next to its benefits mattered as much as the scores. An AI feature that’s confidently wrong is worse than none, and people building accessibility features know that.
  • Presenting along the way meant the final recommendation came with support already behind it.
  • I would build a rough prototype of the top option during the research next time, to check the integration estimates before recommending it.
